The move

Students close their notes and write, sketch or say the key idea from memory for 60 seconds.

Why it works

Pulling information out of memory strengthens it far more than rereading. This is called retrieval practice, and it is among the most studied and reliable ways to improve long-term learning. It also shows students what they actually remember, not what they think they remember.

Watch out for

  • Peeking: notes closed and face down.
  • Grading it: keep it ungraded. The point is the effort to remember.
  • Only doing it right after teaching: the next day is even better.

Level up

  • Partners compare and race to find what both forgot.
  • Bring back an idea from last week too.

Make it work for everyone

  • Multilingual learners: sketches and labels count.
  • IEP and 504 supports: give a word bank or first letters as a cue.
  • Early finishers: write a question about what they remembered.

Tech upgrades

Free tools that change the task, not just the medium.

  • ModifyGoogle Forms brain dumpA short-answer box for the dump. Read the responses to see which key ideas almost nobody remembered.
  • ModifyBlooket or Gimkit (free plans)Assign retrieval practice as a solo game for homework, spaced after the lesson.

AI prompts

Copy, fill in the [brackets] and paste into Gemini, ChatGPT or Claude. Leave out student names and personal details.

Make the materials

I teach [grade] [subject]. We are working on [topic]. List the 8 most important ideas from [lesson or unit]. For each, write one Brain Busters prompt that asks students to recall it from memory (write, sketch or list), plus what a complete answer includes. Before you finish: Prompts must require recall, not recognition: no multiple choice. Write at a level my students can read. If anything above is unclear, ask me up to 3 questions before you start.
