The move

Each student masters one part of a reading with classmates who have the same part, then returns to their group to teach it.

Step by step

Step timing

  1. 1Form home groups1 minute
  2. 2Expert groups8–10 minutes
  3. 4Plan the teach2 minutes
  4. 5Return and teach2 minutes each
  5. 6Quiz3 minutes
  1. Form home groups1 minuteGroups of 3–4. Each member gets a different part: A, B, C or D. Hand out a note-catcher with one row per part.
  2. Expert groups8–10 minutes“All the A’s meet by the window, B’s by the door…”Read the part together and agree on the 3 most important points. Every expert writes the points on their own card.
  3. Check the expertsAbout 30 seconds per group, during expert timeVisit each expert group and read their three points. Fix anything wrong before anyone leaves, then initial the cards.
  4. Plan the teach2 minutes“You’ll have 2 minutes to teach your 3 points. Write one question your home group must answer to show they got it.”
  5. Return and teach2 minutes eachEach expert teaches their home group, then asks their question. Listeners fill in that part’s row on the note-catcher.
  6. Quiz3 minutesOne question per part. Everyone answers alone, using only their note-catcher. If a home group misses a part, that expert reteaches it to them.

At a glance

Time in class
20–30 minutes
Prep
5 minutes: split a reading into 3–4 parts and label them A, B, C, D.
Materials
A text split into parts; index cards; a note-catcher with one row per part
Grouping
Home groups of 3–4, then expert groups
Use it when
A text or topic is too long for everyone to read all of it

See how it works

See it in action

Four animals of the rainforest, one per expert.

Four sections of a chapter on the water cycle.

Four perspectives on the same historical event.

Watch out for

  • Experts who read aloud instead of teaching: notes on one card only.
  • Wrong facts spreading: check and initial every expert card before groups split up.
  • Passive listeners: every listener fills in a note-catcher row, and the quiz covers every part.
  • Uneven parts: balance the length and difficulty.
  • Absent experts: have a spare copy so the group can read that part together.

Level up

  • Set a team goal: every member of the home group scores at least 3 of 4 on the quiz. Celebrate the groups that make it.
  • Experts write a quiz question from their part, and you use the best ones.

Why it works

Explaining something to others is one of the best ways to learn it, and each student holds a piece no one else in their group has, so everyone’s contribution matters. (This structure is often called a jigsaw.) It works when every student is accountable: experts must teach accurately, and every listener is quizzed alone on every part.

Make it work for everyone

  • Multilingual learners: give a shorter or illustrated part; pair with a supportive expert.
  • IEP and 504 supports: offer audio of their part and a note frame.
  • Early finishers: connect their part to another expert’s.

Tech option

Each part as its own linked doc or video clip.

Evidence

Moderate

Roscoe, R. D., & Chi, M. T. H. (2007). Understanding tutor learning: Knowledge-building and knowledge-telling in peer tutors’ explanations and questions. Review of Educational Research, 77(4), 534–574. Students learn by explaining material to peers.

Slavin, R. E. (1995). Cooperative Learning: Theory, Research, and Practice (2nd ed.). Allyn & Bacon. Reviews cooperative structures, including jigsaw.

Johnson, D. W., & Johnson, R. T. (2009). An educational psychology success story: Social interdependence theory and cooperative learning. Educational Researcher, 38(5), 365–379. Cooperative learning works best when each student is individually accountable, not just the group.
