The move

Each week, pick one student and use their interest survey to start short conversations in passing about anything but schoolwork.

Before class: Give the survey

  1. Give the surveyOnce, 10 minutesStudents fill out the interest survey. Keep it private.
  2. Pick your studentMonday, 30 secondsGo down the tracker in order.
  3. Find a detail30 secondsA team, a show, a hobby, something they’re looking forward to.
  4. Start a chat1 minute, a few times that week“Hey, you said you play travel soccer. How’d the game go this weekend?”
  5. Log it10 secondsDate and one note. Move to the next student next week.

Why it works

Students who feel known by a teacher tend to be more engaged and do better over time. In one study, telling teachers and students what they had in common improved their relationships and students’ grades. Short, regular chats are enough to build that connection.

Watch out for

  • Turning it into a school talk: keep it about them, not their grades.
  • Forced conversations: keep it short and natural; a minute is plenty.
  • Skipping quiet students: follow the tracker so no one is missed.
  • Too many students to reach: with several sections, pick one class to start. Add a second student a week if you have the time.

Level up

  • Work through one full class roster, then start the next class.
  • Remember a follow-up detail the next time you see them.

Make it work for everyone

  • Multilingual learners: let them answer the survey in their home language; ask about something from home.
  • IEP and 504 supports: no changes needed; this is about connection.
  • Students who share little: start with a simple choice question: “Pizza or tacos?”

Tech option

Copy the survey questions into a Google Form so answers land in a spreadsheet.

Evidence

Moderate

Gehlbach, H., Brinkworth, M. E., King, A. M., Hsu, L. M., McIntyre, J., & Rogers, T. (2016). Creating birds of similar feathers: Leveraging similarity to improve teacher–student relationships and academic achievement. Journal of Educational Psychology, 108(3), 342–352. Sharing what teachers and students had in common improved relationships and grades.

Hamre, B. K., & Pianta, R. C. (2001). Early teacher–child relationships and the trajectory of children’s school outcomes through eighth grade. Child Development, 72(2), 625–638. The quality of kindergarten teacher–student relationships predicted students’ academic and behavior outcomes through eighth grade.
