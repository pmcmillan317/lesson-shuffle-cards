Try Again

One try. Wrong. Moving on.

12–15 min15-min prepNew card
StepsAt a glanceDiagramExamplesGo deeper
Tap to enlarge

The move

Pair students. One works a short question set. The other holds your answer key, with one hint per question, and says only “yes” or “try again,” never the answer. After two misses on a question, the checker reads that question’s hint from the key. When every answer is right, partners swap roles and use a second question set.

Before class (step 1): Write the question sets and keys

  1. Write the question sets and keysBefore classWrite two short sets of 5–8 questions. For each set, make a key: the answer plus one hint per question. A hint names a first step, not the answer. Print one key per pair (see Printables).
  2. Make pairs and give out roles1 minuteOne student is the solver and gets the question set. The other is the checker and gets the key.“Solvers, you get the questions. Checkers, you hold the key. Don’t let the solver see it.”
  3. Teach the two phrases30 secondsCheckers may say only two things.“Checkers, you can say “yes” or “try again.” Never the answer, and never “close.” Solvers, you can keep trying as long as you like.”
  4. Solve and check5 minutesThe solver works one question and shows or says the answer. The checker compares it to the key. Right: say “yes” and move to the next question. Wrong: say “try again.” Walk around and listen for checkers who say too much.
  5. Read the hint after two missesWhen it happensAfter two misses on the same question, the checker reads that question’s hint from the key. Then the solver tries again.“Two misses? Checker, read the hint. Only the hint.”
  6. Swap and repeat5 minutesWhen every answer in the set is right, partners swap roles and start the second set.“Every answer right? Swap jobs and take the next set.”
  7. Talk it over1 minuteTake a few answers from the room.“Which question took the most tries? What did the hint change?”

Question sets, answer key and hint sheets, pencils or whiteboards.
Grouping
Pairs.

Students stop thinking once an answer is wrong, or you want practice where a miss leads to another try.

See how it works

See it in action

Multiplication facts. For 7 × 8, the hint is: “Think 7 × 4, then double it.”

Solve 2x + 5 = 17. The hint is: “Undo the + 5 first.”

Name the device in “The wind whispered through the trees.” The hint is: “The wind is doing something only a person can do.”

Why it works

A wrong answer often ends the thinking: students wait to be told or give up. Here a wrong answer only means another try, and the hint arrives after two real attempts, so students do the thinking first. The checker has to read the key closely and hold back, which keeps both partners thinking.

Watch out for

  • Checkers who give it away: remind them it is only “yes” or “try again,” with no sighs, hints or “so close.”
  • Hints that give the answer: write each hint as a first step. “Undo the + 5” is a hint. “It’s 6” is not.
  • Lucky guessing: use questions with open answers, not A/B/C/D, so a guess is not worth much.
  • Pairs finishing at different times: early finishers write a new question and hint for the next pair.
  • Keys wandering around the room: collect them at the end.

Level up

  • Run it as a Google Form that won’t accept a wrong answer (setup steps below).
  • Students write the next question set, with answers and hints, for another pair.

Level up setup: a Google Form that won’t accept a wrong answer

This makes a self-grading version. The form plays the checker: a wrong answer gets “Try again” and the student cannot move on until the answer is right.

  1. Make a new form. Open Google Forms and start a blank form. Give it the name of your question set.
  2. Put each question in its own section. Add one question, then use the Add section button before the next one. A student cannot move to the next section until the current question is accepted.
  3. Use a short answer question. Type the question. Turn on Required so a blank answer is not accepted.
  4. Open Response validation. In the question’s three-dot (more options) menu, choose Response validation. Menu labels can shift when Google updates Forms, so look for that wording in the same menu.
  5. Pick a rule that matches the right answer. For a number answer, choose Number and a rule such as Equal to. For a word or phrase, choose Text and a rule such as Contains. To accept several spellings, choose Regular expression and a rule that matches them.
  6. Write the error message. In the custom error text box, type Try again. This message appears when the answer does not match.
  7. Decide how hints work. A form cannot count misses. Either keep the hints on a folded hint sheet students read after two misses, or paste the hint into the error text so it shows after the first miss.
  8. Test it, then share it. Open the preview. Type a wrong answer to check the message, then a right one to check it moves on. Share the link or post it in your class page.

Tip: write your answers as the form will see them. Decide ahead whether 0.5 and 1/2 should both count.

Make it work for everyone

  • Multilingual learners: accept answers in a sketch, a number or a short phrase, and add a picture to the hint.
  • IEP and 504 supports: write hints as a first step to start with, and allow the checker to read the hint after one miss when a plan calls for it.
  • Early finishers: write a harder question with its own hint for the next pair.

Tech upgrades

Free tools that change the task, not just the medium.

  • ModifyGoogle Forms with response validationThe form plays the checker and will not accept a wrong answer. See the setup steps under Level up.
  • AugmentGoogle Slides, hint on the next slideOne question per slide, with its hint on the slide after it. The checker clicks ahead only after two misses.
  • AugmentFlippity flashcardsPut the question on the front and the hint on the back. Students flip only after two misses.

Make the materials

I teach [grade] [subject]. We are working on [topic]. Write a set of 6 short-answer questions on [topic]. For each question give the correct answer and one hint that names a first step without giving away the answer. Put it in a table with the columns Question, Answer and Hint. Before you finish: Make sure each answer is short enough for a partner to check at a glance. Write at a level my students can read. If anything above is unclear, ask me up to 3 questions before you start.
