The move

Before anyone speaks, everyone writes for two minutes: an answer, a reason, and one question they still have. Pairs share from their writing, then the class discusses, and students revise one sentence at the end.

Before class: Write the question

  1. Write the questionBefore classPick an open question with more than one good answer.
  2. Pose it30 secondsPut the question up and say no one talks yet.“Was the treaty fair? Write first, then we talk.”
  3. Silent write2 minutesEveryone writes an answer, a reason, and one question they still have. Circulate and read over shoulders; note two or three ideas to call on.“Pens moving the whole time. Stuck? Start with ‘I think… because…’.”
  4. Pair share2 minutesPartners read their writing aloud, not from memory, then each adds one idea from the other.“Read your reason out loud, word for word.”
  5. Class talk4 minutesCold call from what you read while circulating, including quiet students with strong ideas.“Maya, I read something on your paper I want everyone to hear.”
  6. Revise1 minuteStudents change or add one sentence and mark what changed their thinking.“Change one sentence. Show me what you’d say now.”

Why it works

Writing before talking gives every student think time and a position, so the discussion isn’t decided by the fastest talkers. Putting an idea into words shows students what they do and don’t understand, and writing-to-learn works best when prompts ask students to reflect on their own thinking. Reading papers as you circulate gives you a quick read on the whole class.

Watch out for

  • Writing time turns into talk time: keep it silent and timed, and circulate.
  • Blank pages: post the frame “I think ___ because ___. I still wonder ___.”
  • Pairs summarize from memory: they must read their actual sentence.
  • No time to revise: protect the last minute. It is where students see their thinking change.

Level up

  • Students revise one sentence after the talk.
  • Partners swap papers and write one question in the margin.
  • Collect the writing and sort it to plan tomorrow’s lesson.

Make it work for everyone

  • Multilingual learners: allow writing in the home language or a quick sketch, then share in English with a frame.
  • IEP and 504 supports: offer the sentence frame or speech-to-text.
  • Early finishers: write the strongest argument against your own answer.

Tech upgrades

Free tools that change the task, not just the medium.

  • ModifyClassroom question postTurn on “Students can see classmates’ answers.” They see others’ writing only after they submit their own, so everyone writes first.
  • ModifyGoogle Docs version historyAfter the talk, students revise in the same doc. Version history shows exactly which sentence changed.
  • AugmentVoice typing in Google DocsStudents who think faster than they write get their idea down in time.

Make the materials

I teach [grade] [subject]. We are working on [topic]. Write 4 open questions on [topic] for a write-first discussion. For each: why it has more than one good answer, a strong sample response (answer, reason, remaining question), and a sentence frame for students who are stuck. Before you finish: Make sure students can answer each question using what we have already learned. Write at a level my students can read. If anything above is unclear, ask me up to 3 questions before you start.

Learning science: Writing to learn

Moderate

Bangert-Drowns, R. L., Hurley, M. M., & Wilkinson, B. (2004). The effects of school-based writing-to-learn interventions on academic achievement: A meta-analysis. Review of Educational Research, 74(1), 29–58. Writing to learn had a small positive effect on achievement, and prompts that asked students to reflect on their own understanding worked better.

Graham, S., Kiuhara, S. A., & MacKay, M. (2020). The effects of writing on learning in science, social studies, and mathematics: A meta-analysis. Review of Educational Research, 90(2), 179–226. Writing about content improved learning across all three subjects.

Goodwin, B., Rouleau, K., Abla, C., Baptiste, K., Gibson, T., & Kimball, M. (2022). The New Classroom Instruction That Works (3rd ed.). ASCD. Names cognitive writing as one of the 14 strategies, in the phase where students extend and apply learning.
