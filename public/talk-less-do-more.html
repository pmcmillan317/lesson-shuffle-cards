Talk Less, Do More

Is it mostly you talking?

Whole lessonNo prepPractice-based

The move

You set a talk budget for the period. Five minutes is the classic, and you can set your own. A timer runs whenever you talk. When it hits zero, you can only write on the board or gesture.

Step by step

  1. Set your talk budgetBefore classPick the total minutes you will talk this period. Five minutes is the classic. Choose your own number if that fits your class better. Write it on the board so students can see it.
  2. Tell students the rule30 seconds“I only get five minutes of talking today. I’ll run a timer whenever I talk. When it hits zero, I can only write on the board or use gestures.”
  3. Run the timer whenever you talkAll lessonStart it when you begin talking. Pause it when you stop, and while students work. Use a timer that buzzes at zero. Five minutes is the classic. The Timer button has other lengths.
  4. When it hits zeroFrom then onStop talking, even mid-sentence. For the rest of the period you can only write on the board or gesture. Students keep working from what you wrote.
  5. Look backAfter classCheck how much of your budget you used, and when. Did students need anything you cut?

See how it works

See it in action

Spend part of your budget on one phonics pattern, then students sort word cards. After the buzzer, point to the word wall.

Introduce slope, then pairs find slopes on staircases or ramps in photos. After the buzzer, answer questions by drawing on the board.

Set the historical context, then students analyze a primary source. After the buzzer, write your prompts on the board.

Why it works

Students learn by doing the thinking, not by watching you do it. A review of 225 college STEM studies (Freeman et al., 2014) found students in active classes did better than in lecture classes. Most K–12 evidence is less direct. A timer makes your talk time visible and keeps it short.

Watch out for

  • “Just one more thing”: write it on the board instead of saying it.
  • Students waiting for you to explain again: point to the board or answer with a gesture.
  • Timer running while students talk: pause it. It counts only your talking.
  • No task ready: the move only works if students have something to do after the buzzer.

Level up

  • A student runs the timer.
  • Cut your budget to three minutes.

Make it work for everyone

  • Multilingual learners: post your key points in writing and pair them with gestures, so they stay after you stop talking.
  • IEP and 504 supports: check in one-on-one during the task.
  • Early finishers: have an extension ready so they don’t wait on you.

Tech upgrades

Free tools that change the task, not just the medium.

  • ModifyShort screencasts in Google ClassroomRecord the explanation students may need again. They rewatch it while you coach, and absent students get the real lesson.
  • ModifyGoogle Forms one-question checkAfter you talk, one auto-graded question. The response chart tells you whether to move on or reteach.
  • AugmentClassroomscreenA visible countdown timer keeps you honest, and the class learns to watch it with you.

AI prompts

Copy, fill in the [brackets] and paste into Gemini, ChatGPT or Claude. Leave out student names and personal details.

Make the materials

I teach [grade] [subject]. We are working on [topic]. I will talk for no more than [5] minutes total in this lesson: [paste plan or objective]. List what I should say in that time, what I should write on the board for after the timer hits zero, and what students do the rest of the time. Before you finish: Count the talk time; it must not go over my budget. Write at a level my students can read. If anything above is unclear, ask me up to 3 questions before you start.

Build a classroom app

Build a single-file web app (HTML, CSS and JavaScript in one file) for my [grade] class. Make a "Talk Less" talk-budget timer: I set the total minutes (5 by default). A big Start/Pause button runs the countdown only while I talk. At zero it buzzes and shows "Board and gestures only" full-screen. Requirements: no sign-in, no outside services, nothing saved except in this browser; large text that reads on a projector; works on a Chromebook; every control reachable by keyboard; clean, friendly design. Show me the working app, then tell me how to change the content.

Paste into Gemini, ChatGPT or Claude and check the app before class. You can usually preview it, ask for changes and share it by link.

Evidence

Moderate

Freeman, S., Eddy, S. L., McDonough, M., Smith, M. K., Okoroafor, N., Jordt, H., & Wenderoth, M. P. (2014). Active learning increases student performance in science, engineering, and mathematics. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 111(23), 8410–8415. A review of 225 college studies: students in active-learning classes outperformed those in traditional lectures.

Deslauriers, L., McCarty, L. S., Miller, K., Callaghan, K., & Kestin, G. (2019). Measuring actual learning versus feeling of learning in response to being actively engaged in the classroom. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 116(39), 19251–19257. In a randomized comparison in Harvard intro physics, students who worked problems learned more than students who heard a strong lecture, though they felt they learned less. About 149 college students in one subject.
