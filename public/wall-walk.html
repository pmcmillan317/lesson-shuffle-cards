Wall Walk

Share-outs that drag

The move

Post four to six prompts or pieces of student work around the room. Each small group starts at a different one and writes a response. On your signal groups rotate, adding to or pushing back on what earlier groups wrote. Groups finish back at their first stop and share what changed.

Step by step

  2. 3Write and rotate90 seconds per stop
  3. 4Return to the first stop2 minutes
  4. 5Share what changed2–3 minutes

Before class (step 1): Post the prompts

  1. Post the promptsBefore classPut four to six prompts or pieces of student work on chart paper or sheets around the room. Leave space to write under each. One group will visit each stop, so match stops to groups.
  2. Send groups to their first stop1 minuteMake small groups, one per stop. Give each group a marker color so you can see who wrote what.“Go to the stop I point you to. Read it, then write your response together.”“When I give the signal, move to the next stop clockwise. Read what is there. Add to it or push back.”
  3. Write and rotate90 seconds per stopGroups write at their stop. Give the signal and everyone moves to the next stop in the same direction. Repeat until each group has been to every stop.“Time. Move to your next stop.”
  4. Return to the first stop2 minutesGroups end back where they started. They read everything added to their first response.“Back at your first stop. Read what others added. Circle the comment that changed your thinking most.”
  5. Share what changed2–3 minutesEach group shares one thing that changed.“What did you start with? What changed after other groups added to it?”

At a glance

Time in class
12–15 minutes with five stops. Add or take away 90 seconds for each stop.
Prep
About 10 minutes: write four to six prompts on chart paper or sheets and post them. Prompt slips are under Printables.
Materials
Chart paper or sheets, markers in different colors, tape.
Grouping
Small groups of three or four, one group per stop.
Use it when
Whole-class share-outs drag and only a few students talk.

See how it works

See it in action

Five pictures of animal homes. Each group adds one animal that could live there and why.

Four photos from a primary source set. Groups add what they see and a question they would ask.

Six claims about a novel’s theme. Groups add evidence or push back with a counterexample.

Why it works

In a whole-class share-out a few students talk while everyone else waits. On the wall every group writes at once, and the paper keeps a record of the conversation. Each group has to read what others wrote before adding to it, and pushing back on written ideas keeps the focus on the idea, not on the person.

Watch out for

  • Crowding at one stop: one group per stop. If you have more groups than stops, add a stop.
  • Copying earlier answers: require each new group to add something new or push back.
  • Prompts with one right answer: use open prompts so each group has something to add.
  • Writing too small to read: use markers and write big.
  • Rotation getting stuck: point out the direction and use one clear signal.

Level up

  • One student stays behind at each stop to explain it to the next group.
  • Use pieces of student work instead of prompts and have groups leave feedback.

Make it work for everyone

  • Multilingual learners: allow drawing and home-language notes, and post starters: “I agree because…”, “I would add…”, “I disagree because…”
  • IEP and 504 supports: give each group member a job, such as reader, writer or timekeeper, and let them speak the response while a partner writes.
  • Early finishers: circle the strongest idea on your stop and say why.

Tech upgrades

Free tools that change the task, not just the medium.

  • AugmentGoogle Slides, one slide per promptGroups type on a shared slide at each stop. Version history shows how the thinking built.
  • ModifyPadlet (shelf format, free plan)One column per prompt. Groups post to a column, then add comments or push back on earlier posts.
  • RedefineOne Padlet across class periodsLater periods add to the same prompts, so the conversation builds over the whole day.

Write at a level my students can read. If anything above is unclear, ask me up to 3 questions before you start.
