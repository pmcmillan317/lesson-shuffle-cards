The move

Before you start, draw a line on the board with three points: yesterday, today and later. Students write one way today connects to yesterday. At the end, they guess what goes under “later”: where does this lead next? Hear two guesses.

  1. Draw the line20 secondsDraw a line on the board with three points. Label them Yesterday, Today and Later.“Here is our line. Yesterday, today and later.”
  2. Name today20 secondsWrite today’s topic at the Today point. Write last class’s topic at Yesterday.“Today we are learning ___.”
  3. Students write the link1 minuteStudents write one sentence on paper or a mini whiteboard. Ask for the specific idea, not “we learned something.”“Write one sentence: how does today connect to yesterday?”
  4. Hear the links30 secondsTake one or two sentences. Leave the Later point blank for now.“Hold on to Later. You’ll fill it in at the end.”
  5. Teach the lessonYour lessonLeave the line on the board. Point to it if the lesson drifts.
  6. Point back to the line1 minuteNear the end, stand by the line. Students write a guess for the Later point. Hear two guesses, then add where it really leads.“Look at the line. What goes under Later? Where does today lead next?”

At a glance

Time in class
3–5 minutes, split between the start and end of the lesson.
Prep
None. Draw the line on the board as class begins.
Materials
Board or chart paper, marker. Paper or mini whiteboards for students.
Grouping
Whole class, students writing on their own.
Use it when
A lesson feels disconnected from the last one, or a new unit or topic starts.

See how it works

See it in action

Yesterday: adding to 10. Today: adding to 20. Later: adding tens and ones, then money.

Yesterday: parts of a cell. Today: how cells get energy. Later: how body systems work together.

Yesterday: causes of World War I. Today: the Treaty of Versailles. Later: how it shaped what came next.

Why it works

When every lesson stands alone, students have nothing to attach new ideas to. Writing the link to yesterday asks them to recall it, and showing where today leads gives the work a purpose. Ending at the same line keeps students thinking about what comes next.

Watch out for

  • Vague links (“we learned stuff”): ask “Which idea from yesterday?”
  • A Later point that is too far away: name something students will actually do next week.
  • An erased line: keep it up until the end of class, or put it on chart paper.
  • Skipping the ending: set a reminder to point back to the line before the bell.

Level up

  • A student draws and explains the line tomorrow.
  • Keep one running line on the wall for the whole unit and add a point each day.

Make it work for everyone

  • Multilingual learners: give a starter: “Yesterday we ___. Today we ___.” Allow a sketch.
  • IEP and 504 supports: post two or three choices for the yesterday topic so students pick instead of recall from scratch.
  • Early finishers: write a second guess for the Later point and say why.

Make the materials

I teach [grade] [subject]. We are working on [unit]. Yesterday we covered [topic]. Today we cover [topic]. Write one plain sentence linking today to yesterday, one sentence on where it leads next week, one on where it leads later in the year, and one closing question that asks what comes next. Before you finish: Keep each sentence under 20 words. Write at a level my students can read. If anything above is unclear, ask me up to 3 questions before you start.
