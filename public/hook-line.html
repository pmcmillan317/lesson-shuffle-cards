The move

Open class with a question students want answered, and hold back the answer until the lesson gives it.

Step by step

Before class: Pick your hook

  1. Pick your hookBefore classChoose one hook type from the menu below. Then finish this sentence: “This hook leads to today’s big idea, which is ___.” If you can’t finish it, pick a different hook.
  2. Show the hook30 secondsPut the image, puzzle or question up as students walk in. No directions yet.
  3. Guess1 minute“Write down your best guess. No wrong answers yet.”
  4. Share two guesses1 minuteTake two or three. Don’t confirm or correct.“Hold onto that. By the end of class you’ll know.”
  5. TeachLessonPoint back to the hook when the lesson reaches the answer.
  6. RevealLast 5 minutes“Who was closest? What made you think that?”

Pick your hook

Five kinds of hooks, adapted from the “cognitive interest cues” in The New Classroom Instruction That Works. Pick the one that fits today’s content.

Mystery

Use it when you can show part of something and hide the rest.

  • Elementary: Close-up of a butterfly wing: “What is this?”
  • Middle: A map with one country’s name covered: “Where is this? How do you know?”
  • High: A 1920s ad with the product hidden: “What were they selling?”

Surprise

Use it when a result breaks what students expect. Have them predict first.

  • Elementary: An orange floats. Peel it and it sinks. Why?
  • Middle: A can of diet soda floats; a regular one sinks. Why?
  • High: Predict, then drop a heavy ball and a light ball at the same time.

Debate

Use it when reasonable people disagree, and today’s content helps settle it.

  • Elementary: “Should recess be longer if the school day is longer?”
  • Middle: “Should our town ban gas leaf blowers?”
  • High: “Should the voting age be 16?”

Gap

Use it when students know a lot, but not quite enough to solve this.

  • Elementary: “Share 3 brownies fairly among 4 friends.”
  • Middle: “You know speed is distance divided by time. So how fast is the Moon moving around Earth?”
  • High: “You can find the area of a rectangle. What about the area under this curve?”

Close to home

Use it when you can tie the content to a local place or something students care about.

  • Elementary: “How many steps from our door to the cafeteria? How could we find out faster?”
  • Middle: “Is the walk signal by our school long enough to cross?”
  • High: “Why does your phone battery drain faster in the cold?”

Why it works

Curiosity is a gap between what we know and what we want to know. When students are curious, they pay closer attention and remember more of what comes next. The New Classroom Instruction That Works calls these openers cognitive interest cues: a mystery, a surprise, a debate, a gap in what students know, or a link to their own lives.

The hook has to serve the lesson. Research on “seductive details” finds that interesting material unrelated to the main idea pulls attention away from it and lowers learning. That’s why you check the hook against today’s big idea before class.

Watch out for

  • Fun but off-topic hooks: the answer must come from today’s content. A flashy hook that doesn’t lead to the big idea can actually lower learning.
  • Revealing too early: the payoff is the wait.
  • Long discussions: cap it at three minutes.

Level up

  • Don’t reveal the answer until the last five minutes.
  • Let students vote on the most convincing guess before the reveal.

Make it work for everyone

  • Multilingual learners: image hooks need no reading.
  • IEP and 504 supports: let students point or circle on a printed copy.
  • Early finishers: write a second question the hook makes them wonder.

Tech option

Use a slide that zooms out from a close-up, or a short muted video clip.

Evidence

Moderate

Gruber, M. J., Gelman, B. D., & Ranganath, C. (2014). States of curiosity modulate hippocampus-dependent learning via the dopaminergic circuit. Neuron, 84(2), 486–496. People remembered more when they were curious, including unrelated information shown during the curious state.

Goodwin, B., Rouleau, K., Abla, C., Baptiste, K., Gibson, T., & Kimball, M. (2022). The New Classroom Instruction That Works (3rd ed.). ASCD. Describes cognitive interest cues (mysteries, controversies, surprising problems and real-world connections) as the strategy for helping students become interested in learning.

Loewenstein, G. (1994). The psychology of curiosity: A review and reinterpretation. Psychological Bulletin, 116(1), 75–98. Curiosity arises from a gap between what we know and what we want to know.

Sundararajan, N., & Adesope, O. (2020). Keep it coherent: A meta-analysis of the seductive details effect. Educational Psychology Review, 32(3), 707–734. Interesting but irrelevant details reduce learning, which is why the hook must connect to the lesson’s main idea.

Find the research: Gruber (2014) · Goodwin (2022) · Loewenstein (1994) · Sundararajan (2020)
