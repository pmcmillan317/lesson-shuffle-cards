The move

Each pair learns one subtopic fast, then teaches it to the class in exactly two minutes.

Step by step

Step timing

  1. 1Assign1 minute
  2. 2Research10 minutes
  3. 5Present2 minutes each
  4. 6Capture30 seconds after each talk
  5. 7Quiz3 minutes
  1. Assign1 minuteGive each pair a subtopic and one guiding question. Hand every student a takeaway sheet with one row per talk.
  2. Research10 minutes“Find the three things your classmates most need to know. Both of you must be ready to give the whole talk. I’ll pick who starts.”
  3. Check the factsAbout 30 seconds per pair, during researchCirculate and read each pair’s three points before they plan the talk. Fix anything wrong now, then initial the card. No initials, no talk.
  4. PlanLast 2 minutes of researchPairs decide who says what and write one question for the class. Notes on one index card only.
  5. Present2 minutes eachRoll a die or pull a name stick to choose which partner starts. Start the timer. When it beeps, they stop mid-sentence.“Time! Big applause.”
  6. Capture30 seconds after each talkEvery listener writes the talk’s most important point on their takeaway sheet. The pair asks its question, and you pull a name to answer it.
  7. Quiz3 minutesOne question from each talk. Everyone answers alone, using only their takeaway sheet. Look at which talk’s question the class missed most and reteach that point tomorrow.

At a glance

Time in class
About 20–25 minutes for 5–6 pairs (10 research, 2½ per talk, 3 for the quiz). Run talks over two days for bigger classes.
Prep
5 minutes: list one subtopic per pair and make a takeaway sheet with one row per talk.
Materials
Textbook, articles or devices; a timer; index cards; a takeaway sheet for each student
Grouping
Pairs
Use it when
A topic splits into parts students can learn on their own

Why it works

Preparing to explain something to others makes students organize and question what they’ve learned, and explaining it out loud deepens it further. The hard time limit forces them to choose what matters most. Group work pays off when every student is accountable for learning, so here either partner can be called to present and every listener is quizzed on every talk.

Watch out for

  • Reading from notes: one index card only.
  • Overruns: the timer is the game. Stop them every time.
  • A passive audience: every listener fills in a takeaway row for every talk, and the quiz draws on those notes.
  • Wrong facts spreading: no pair presents without your initials on their card.
  • One partner doing all the work: choose who starts at random, so both have to prepare the whole talk.

Level up

  • The audience asks one question per talk, and presenters answer without notes.
  • Cut the time to 90 seconds.

Make it work for everyone

  • Multilingual learners: allow one visual and a sentence frame: “The most important thing about ___ is…”
  • IEP and 504 supports: offer a shorter text and let the partner do more of the speaking.
  • Early finishers: prepare a one-question quiz for their talk.

Roscoe, R. D., & Chi, M. T. H. (2007). Understanding tutor learning: Knowledge-building and knowledge-telling in peer tutors’ explanations and questions. Review of Educational Research, 77(4), 534–574. Students learn by explaining, especially when they reflect on and build on what they know rather than just repeating it.

Johnson, D. W., & Johnson, R. T. (2009). An educational psychology success story: Social interdependence theory and cooperative learning. Educational Researcher, 38(5), 365–379. Cooperative learning works best when each student is individually accountable, not just the group.
