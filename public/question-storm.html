Question Storm

Silence after “Any questions?”

5–8 min5-min prepPractice-based

The move

Show an image, quote or graph. For five minutes, pairs ask questions while a student lists them on the board. Nobody answers yet. When the lesson answers a question, a student stands up and erases it. Leftover questions open tomorrow.

Step by step

Before class (step 1): Pick a stimulus

  1. Pick a stimulusBefore class (5 minutes)Choose one image, quote or graph that connects to today’s topic. Put it on a slide or the board. Check the question starters below for the kind of stimulus you picked.
  2. Show it30 secondsShow the stimulus. For a picture, have students look at it one quarter at a time. For a quote, ask who wrote it, and why.“Look closely. In a minute, pairs will ask questions. Only questions.”
  3. Set the rules30 secondsPick a student to be the writer at the board. Tell the class the rules.“Ask as many questions as you can. Don’t answer any of them. The writer lists each one exactly as it’s asked.”
  4. Ask questions4 minutesPairs ask each other questions and call them out. The writer lists each one on the board. If anyone starts answering, remind them: questions only.“Go! Keep them coming.”
  5. Add one strong question1 minuteEach pair adds one strong question of its own, one that would make you want to learn more.“Add one strong question of your own. Make it one that really makes you wonder.”
  6. Erase as the lesson answersDuring the lessonWhen the lesson answers a question on the board, that question’s asker stands up and erases it. Point back to the board as you teach.
  7. Leftovers open tomorrowNext class (2 minutes)Leave the unanswered questions up, or photograph them. Start the next class with them.“These are still here. Which one should we chase?”

Why it works

Asking questions makes students active. Students who ask their own questions have a reason to care about the answers. The board shows you what they already know and wonder, and erasing each question as the lesson answers it shows them the lesson is working for them.

Watch out for

  • Students answering each other: remind them “questions only” and keep the clock running.
  • Erasing too soon: erase only when the lesson really answers the question, not when someone guesses.
  • Statements disguised as questions: ask the writer to turn them into questions before they go on the board.
  • Stalling: give a starter, such as “Why…? What if…? How does…?” (more under Question starters).

Question starters by stimulus

  • Picture: look at one quarter at a time. “What do you see here? What is missing? What happened just before?”
  • Quote or text: “Who wrote it, and why? Who was it written for? What does it leave out?”
  • Graph: “What is being measured? What changed, and when? What would we need to know to explain it?”
  • Stuck pairs: give a starter such as “Why…? What if…? How does…?”

Ways to run it

  • Sticky notes: pairs write each question on a sticky note and stick it on the board. When the lesson answers one, its pair peels it off.
  • Tech version: students type questions into a shared Slides deck or Padlet while a student at the front shows them on the screen. Delete a question when it is answered.
  • Think-pair-share: students write two questions alone for one minute, trade with a partner for one minute, then each pair calls out its best ones for the writer to list.

Level up

  • The class picks one leftover question to chase.
  • Groups predict how they’d find the answer to one leftover question.

Make it work for everyone

  • Multilingual learners: post question starters in English and home languages.
  • IEP and 504 supports: let students say questions while a partner writes, or write them on sticky notes.
  • Early finishers: choose the three questions they most want answered and say why.

Tech upgrades

Free tools that change the task, not just the medium.

  • AugmentAnswerGarden or Mentimeter (free plans)Every question appears on screen as it’s typed, anonymously, so quiet students add as many as anyone.
  • ModifyGoogle SheetsPaste the questions in, add a column for “answered in class,” and vote on leftovers with checkboxes.
  • ModifyPadlet that lasts the unitPost the leftover questions; students add evidence under them as they find answers over the following weeks.

AI prompts

Copy, fill in the [brackets] and paste into Gemini, ChatGPT or Claude. Leave out student names and personal details.

Make the materials

I teach [grade] [subject]. We are working on [topic]. Give me 3 possible Question Storm stimuli for [topic]: an image idea, a short quote, and a graph idea. For each, list 5 questions students are likely to ask, and mark which ones today’s lesson would answer. Before you finish: Stimuli must not contain questions themselves; they should spark questions. Write at a level my students can read. If anything above is unclear, ask me up to 3 questions before you start.

Build a classroom app

Build a single-file web app (HTML, CSS and JavaScript in one file) for my [grade] class. Make a question board: students type questions one by one, each appears as a card, I can click a card to mark it answered and it fades out, and a final view shows only the unanswered questions so the class can vote on one to chase tomorrow. Requirements: no sign-in, no outside services, nothing saved except in this browser; large text that reads on a projector; works on a Chromebook; every control reachable by keyboard; clean, friendly design. Show me the working app, then tell me how to change the content.

Paste into Gemini, ChatGPT or Claude and check the app before class. You can usually preview it, ask for changes and share it by link.

Evidence

Moderate

Rothstein, D., & Santana, L. (2011). Make Just One Change: Teach Students to Ask Their Own Questions. Harvard Education Press. Describes the Question Formulation Technique, a longer routine that inspired this card.

Rosenshine, B., Meister, C., & Chapman, S. (1996). Teaching students to generate questions: A review of the intervention studies. Review of Educational Research, 66(2), 181–221. Teaching students to generate their own questions raised reading comprehension scores in the studies reviewed. The studies used reading passages, not images or clips.
