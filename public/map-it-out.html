The move

Students map 10–12 key terms from memory, drawing arrows between them and labeling every arrow with the relationship.

  1. Post the terms30 secondsNotes closed.
  2. Model one link1 minuteDraw two terms with an arrow and a label.“Every arrow needs words on it, like ‘causes,’ ‘is part of,’ ‘leads to.’”
  3. Map from memory5 minutes“Place all the terms. Connect them. Label every arrow.”
  4. Check2 minutesOpen notes. Fix or add links in a different color.
  5. Compare2 minutesPartners find one link the other didn’t make.

Why it works

A concept map makes students show how ideas connect, not just what they are. Doing it from memory adds retrieval practice, one of the strongest boosts to long-term learning. One study found that recalling information beat making a concept map with notes open, so the “from memory” part matters.

Watch out for

  • Unlabeled arrows: the label is where the thinking shows.
  • Copying from notes: map first, check second.
  • Too many terms: 10–12 is plenty.

Level up

  • Add a term they haven’t learned yet and predict where it fits.
  • Find the one term that connects to the most others and explain why.

Make it work for everyone

  • Multilingual learners: give a bank of linking words.
  • IEP and 504 supports: provide terms on cut-out slips to arrange first.
  • Early finishers: add two terms of their own.

Evidence

Strong

Nesbit, J. C., & Adesope, O. O. (2006). Learning with concept and knowledge maps: A meta-analysis. Review of Educational Research, 76(3), 413–448. Concept mapping improved learning compared with reading, lists and lecture.

Karpicke, J. D., & Blunt, J. R. (2011). Retrieval practice produces more learning than elaborative studying with concept mapping. Science, 331(6018), 772–775. Recalling from memory beat mapping with materials open, which is why this card maps from memory.
