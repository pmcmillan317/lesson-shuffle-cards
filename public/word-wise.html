The move

Teach 3–5 key words the way they stick: say it, a kid-friendly meaning, an example and a non-example, then students use it in a sentence about their own lives. Bring each word back twice this week.

Step by step

Step timing, for each word

  1. 2Say it15 seconds
  2. 3Mean it30 seconds
  3. 4Example, non-example30 seconds
  4. 5Use it45 seconds

Before class: Pick 3–5 words

  1. Pick 3–5 wordsBefore classChoose words students will meet again across texts and subjects, not rare ones. For each, write a kid-friendly meaning, one example and one non-example.
  2. Say it15 secondsSay the word clearly. Students repeat it twice and write it down.“Say it with me: migrate.”
  3. Mean it30 secondsGive the meaning in everyday words, not the dictionary’s.“Migrate means to move a long way, usually at the same time every year.”
  4. Example, non-example30 secondsShow one of each. Students give a thumbs up or down and say why.“Geese flying south every fall: migrate or not? A dog running to the park?”
  5. Use it45 secondsEveryone writes one sentence that links the word to their own life, then reads it to a partner, who checks it fits the meaning. Cold call two or three.“Use migrate in a sentence about you, your family or something you’ve seen.”
  6. Revisit twiceLater this weekBring each word back for two minutes on two more days: a which-one-fits question, a picture to match, or a sentence to finish.“Which word fits: the whales ___ to warmer water every winter?”

See it in action

Before a read-aloud: enormous, cautious, delighted. Non-example for cautious: running into the street without looking.

Science: evidence, predict, adapt. Non-example for evidence: “I just think so.”

History: propaganda, sovereignty, boycott. Non-example for boycott: buying more of a product to show support.

Why it works

Students need several meaningful encounters with a word before they own it. A plain-language meaning, a contrast between example and non-example, and a sentence about their own lives make the word stick, and spaced revisits stop it fading. Choosing words that show up across many texts gives the biggest payoff for the time.

Watch out for

  • Dictionary definitions: rewrite the meaning in words students already know.
  • Too many words: cap it at five. A list of twenty gets copied, not learned.
  • One and done: words fade without revisits. Put the two revisits in your plan book now.
  • Only the quick students share: everyone writes a sentence before anyone speaks, then cold call.

Level up

  • Students sketch the word next to their sentence.
  • Word ladder: rank three related words by strength, such as warm, hot, scorching.
  • Students hunt for the word in reading or conversation and report back.

Make it work for everyone

  • Multilingual learners: show a picture, and point out a home-language cognate when there is one (migrate / migrar).
  • IEP and 504 supports: give a sentence frame: “One time I ___ when ___.”
  • Early finishers: write one sentence that uses two of today’s words.

Tech option

Keep one slide per word with a picture and the best student sentences, then reuse it for the revisits.

Evidence

Strong

Beck, I. L., McKeown, M. G., & Kucan, L. (2013). Bringing Words to Life: Robust Vocabulary Instruction (2nd ed.). Guilford Press. Student-friendly explanations, examples and non-examples, and many encounters with each word.

Marulis, L. M., & Neuman, S. B. (2010). The effects of vocabulary intervention on young children’s word learning: A meta-analysis. Review of Educational Research, 80(3), 300–335. Vocabulary instruction produced meaningful gains in word learning. Most studies were with preschool and kindergarten children.

Goodwin, B., Rouleau, K., Abla, C., Baptiste, K., Gibson, T., & Kimball, M. (2022). The New Classroom Instruction That Works (3rd ed.). ASCD. Names vocabulary instruction as one of the 14 strategies, in the phase where students focus on new learning.
