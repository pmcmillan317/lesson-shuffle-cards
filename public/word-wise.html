Recite the word, can’t use it?

The move

Pick up to four key words and hand out the Frayer sheet. For each word, students write the meaning in their own words, sketch it, and add an example and a near-miss: something close that isn’t it. Partners compare. Bring each word back for two short revisits.

Before class (step 1): Pick up to four words

  1. Pick up to four wordsBefore class (5 minutes)Choose words students will meet again, not rare ones. One sheet holds four words, two per side. Jot your own meaning, example and near-miss for each so you can check student work. For science and technical words, see “Examples and near-misses for technical words” below.
  2. Hand out the Frayer sheet1 minuteOne per student. Tell them the four boxes for each word: meaning in your own words, a sketch, an example and a near-miss.“A near-miss is something close to the word that isn’t it.”
  3. Say the word15 secondsSay the word clearly. Students repeat it twice and write it in the middle of a Frayer model.“Say it with me: migrate.”
  4. Meaning in their own words30 secondsStudents write what the word means in their own words, not the dictionary’s.“What does migrate mean? Write it your way.”
  5. Sketch it30 secondsStudents draw a quick picture of the word. Stick figures are fine.
  6. Example and near-miss30 secondsStudents add one example and one near-miss: something close that isn’t the word.“Geese flying south every fall is an example. What is close to migrate but isn’t it?”
  7. Partners compare15 secondsPartners compare all four boxes. Then give your own meaning so students can fix their sheet. Repeat steps 3 to 7 for each word.“Same meaning? Does your partner’s near-miss really not fit?”
  8. Revisit twiceTwo more days, 2 minutes eachBring each word back for two minutes on two later days: a which-one-fits question, a picture to match, or a sentence to finish.“Which word fits: the whales ___ to warmer water every winter?”

See how it works

See it in action

Before a read-aloud: enormous, cautious, delighted. Near-miss for cautious: scared (afraid to try anything, which is more than careful).

Science: evidence, predict, adapt. Near-miss for evidence: an opinion that sounds sure, like “Everybody knows it.”

History: propaganda, sovereignty, boycott. Near-miss for boycott: a protest sign, since it speaks out but doesn’t stop buying.

Why it works

Students need several meaningful encounters with a word before they own it. Putting the meaning in their own words, drawing it, and comparing an example with a near-miss make the word stick, and spaced revisits stop it fading. Choosing words that show up across many texts gives the biggest payoff for the time.

Watch out for

  • Dictionary definitions: tell students to put the meaning in words they would use with a friend.
  • No obvious near-miss: use a look-alike from the same family, such as mitochondria vs. chloroplast or mammal vs. reptile.
  • Too many words: cap it at four. A list of twenty gets copied, not learned.
  • One and done: words fade without revisits. Put the two revisits in your plan book now.
  • One partner doing all the talking: each partner points to one box on the other’s sheet that is different, and says why.

Examples and near-misses for technical words

The example and near-miss boxes are the hardest part for science and technical words. Use these moves when students are stuck.

  • Near-miss = a look-alike from the same family. Mitochondria: a chloroplast is also a part inside a cell, but it makes food instead of releasing energy. Mammal: a reptile is also a backbone animal, but it is cold-blooded and lays eggs.
  • Ask “What is it most often confused with?” That is usually the best near-miss. Weather and climate, simile and metaphor, evidence and opinion.
  • Make the example concrete. Name a real one students can picture. Mitochondria: the part of a muscle cell that releases energy when you run.
  • Say why it misses. Students name the one feature that makes the near-miss not the word.

Level up

  • Partners guess the word from the sketch alone.
  • Word ladder: rank three related words by strength, such as warm, hot, scorching.
  • Students hunt for the word in reading or conversation and report back.

Make it work for everyone

  • Multilingual learners: show a picture, and point out a home-language cognate when there is one (migrate / migrar).
  • IEP and 504 supports: give a sentence frame for the near-miss: “It is close to ___, but it isn’t because ___.”
  • Early finishers: write one sentence that uses two of today’s words.

Make the materials

I teach [grade] [subject]. We are working on [topic]. Here are my words: [list]. For each word give: a kid-friendly meaning in everyday words; one example; one near-miss (something close that is not the word; for a technical word, a look-alike from the same family, like mitochondria vs. chloroplast, with the reason it misses); a simple sketch idea; and one which-word-fits sentence with a blank for the revisit. Put it in a table I can paste into a slide. Before you finish: Make sure each near-miss is close to the word but clearly not it, for a reason students can name. Write at a level my students can read. If anything above is unclear, ask me up to 3 questions before you start.

Build a classroom app

Build a single-file web app (HTML, CSS and JavaScript in one file) for my [grade] class. Make a vocabulary revisit game: I enter words and meanings. Round 1 shows a meaning and four word buttons; round 2 shows a sentence with a blank. Track the score for the class, and at the end list which words were missed most so I know what to reteach. Requirements: no sign-in, no outside services, nothing saved except in this browser; large text that reads on a projector; works on a Chromebook; every control reachable by keyboard; clean, friendly design. Show me the working app, then tell me how to change the content.

Paste into Gemini, ChatGPT or Claude and check the app before class. You can usually preview it, ask for changes and share it by link.

Evidence

Moderate

Beck, I. L., McKeown, M. G., & Kucan, L. (2013). Bringing Words to Life: Robust Vocabulary Instruction (2nd ed.). Guilford Press. Student-friendly explanations, examples and non-examples, and many encounters with each word.

Marulis, L. M., & Neuman, S. B. (2010). The effects of vocabulary intervention on young children’s word learning: A meta-analysis. Review of Educational Research, 80(3), 300–335. Vocabulary instruction produced meaningful gains in word learning. Most studies were with preschool and kindergarten children.
